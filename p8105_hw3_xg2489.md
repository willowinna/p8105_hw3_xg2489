``` r
data("ny_noaa")
```

The NOAA New York weather dataset contains daily meteorological records
across multiple weather stations in New York from 1981 to 2010. The
dataset has approximately 2595176 rows (daily observations) and 7
initial columns: `id`, `date`, `prcp`, `snow`, `snwd`, `tmax`, `tmin`.

Missing values are important in this dataset. Many rows contain NA
values for temperature and snow/precipitation measurements, meaning some
daily records lack valid readings. In `tmax`and `tmin`, over 40 percent
of the record is missing which may affect the related plots.

We split `date` into `year`, `month`, and `day` for future analysis.
Among the variables, units of `prcp`, `tmax`, `tmin` need to be divided
by 10 because their units are tenth of the reasonable units.

For snow value count, the majority of daily observations have
`snow = 0`, meaning no snowfall on those days. We also observe an
negative value of snowfall (`snow = -13`) which represents an erroneous
data entry and it should be excluded from subsequent analysis.

``` r
nhanes_df =
```

First, we import the datasets and use `janitor::clean_names` to
standardize the variable names, for `covar_df`, first 4 rows are skipped
as it is descriptive content. Then we combine 2 datasets by `seqn` and
exclude 22 participants less than 21 years of ages and those with
missing demographic data. Variables `sex` and `education` are encoded to
factors (ordered factors for `education`).

These scatter plots display the relationship between total daily MIMS
activity and age, separated by education level and colored by sex.
Across all 3 education groups, total daily physical activity generally
declines with increasing age.

Sex differences in activity levels vary across educational backgrounds.
For individuals with a high school education or higher, females
consistently maintain higher average MIMS activity levels than males
across almost all age groups. While in the ‘Less than high school’
group, males and females show similar levels during early adulthood and
among participants older than 40, males have higher activity levels than
females.

Education appears to buffer against early-age activity decline.
Participants in the ‘More than high school’ category display a
relatively plateaued activity level between ages 20 and 60 for both
males and females. In contrast, those with ‘Less than high school’ or
‘High school equivalent’ education demonstrate earlier peaks and sharper
drops in activity.

This figure shows loess-smoothed curves of minute-level MIMS activity
values over the 24-hour day, faceted by education level and colored by
sex. Across all education levels, activity is lowest during sleep
(before 5 AM) and increases up rapidly starting at 5 AM as people wake
up and begin their day.

Women generally show higher activity levels during the day than men in
most groups. For people with a high school equivalent or higher
education, women maintain noticeably higher activity values from morning
until evening. However, in the ‘Less than high school’ group, men and
women follow almost identical daytime patterns, with women only showing
a slightly higher peak around noon.

Educational background changes how daytime activity is distributed.
People with less than high school education have a single sharp peak
around midday followed by a steady drop in the afternoon. In contrast,
those with higher education maintain a steady, high-level plateau of
activity throughout normal working hours (from 10 AM to 6 PM).
